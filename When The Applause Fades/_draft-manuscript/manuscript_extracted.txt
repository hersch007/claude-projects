Chapter 1
One day I hope somebody will see The woman behind this melody. - Nova Hart, from “Lonely Light" 5 weeks at #1
ELLIOT
***
Elliot Brooks closed the laptop the moment the advertisement flashed across the screen.
Meet mature singles in your area.
At fifty-five, after five years of widowhood and his grown daughter’s ongoing attempts to “help,” the internet had decided he required help too.
The house still held a whisper of coffee from earlier that morning. Beyond the back windows, the ocean moved in slow gray-blue lines beneath an overcast sky. Early April couldn’t decide what season it wanted to be. 
In another month, Oceanside’s boardwalk would fill with tourists and rental bikes and sunburned families. For now, the town still belonged to the locals.
Saturday was uncomplicated—Scripture, coffee, maybe a walk on the beach if the weather behaved.
Not camera crews.
Not a global pop star standing on his porch.
All because he clicked the wrong advertisement while checking stock reports three weeks earlier.
Elliot adjusted the cuffs of his shirt as he crossed the foyer, already trying to think of a polite way to survive whatever this experience was supposed to be.
The doorbell rang.
Too cheerful. Too loud.
He opened the door.
And there she was.
Nova Hart.
Until she stepped inside, Elliot had never noticed how much noise the old house made.
Not because she was louder than the room.
Because nothing about her belonged to rooms this quiet.
The woman before him looked smaller than the version the world projected onto stadium screens.
Not less striking.
Just more real.
A tall man lingered near the porch steps in a dark windbreaker, alert and composed, watching the street with practiced discretion.
“We’re clear,” he said.
“Thank you, Reid,” she replied, her voice low and calm.
The man gave Elliot a brief nod before stepping back toward the SUV.
“Security,” Nova said, sounding apologetic. “Reid distrusts anything without metal detectors.”
“Reasonable philosophy these days,” Elliot replied.
That earned the first real smile he’d seen from her.
She stepped inside with the calm ease of someone long accustomed to cameras and strangers. A trace of cool ocean air followed her into the foyer before fading. The denim jacket she wore looked softened with use rather than styled for effect.
Her boots tapped across the hardwood.
When her eyes met his, he forgot whatever polite thing he had been about to say.
He had expected someone younger, somehow.
Less composed.
Less… present.
The next voice arrived before he could think too hard about that.
“This light is incredible.”
A woman swept through the doorway, carrying both a tablet and enough energy for the entire group. Her eyes began tracking windows, angles, and reflections.
“Right here by the staircase,” she announced. “The people who built this house apparently respected natural lighting.”
Nova gave Marcy a look that suggested she’d heard this speech before.
“Most houses ignore their potential,” Marcy said.
The woman turned toward him and smiled.
“Marcy,” she said, glancing past him toward the growing commotion in the foyer. “If somebody starts moving furniture without asking, let me know before Nova sees it.”
Elliot stepped aside before the rest of the production team ended up standing on his porch.
“Nice to meet you.”
“Promise we’ll be quick,” Marcy said, already adjusting a camera lens.
Behind them, the rest of the crew entered with a steady wave of movement and equipment.
Cases thumped onto hardwood floors. Light stands unfolded. Voices overlapped.
Elliot’s foyer no longer felt entirely like his.
The photographer lifted his camera.
“There we go, Mr. Brooks. Relax those shoulders. Just like you’re enjoying this.”
Elliot exhaled.
Be still.
He let the breath go.
His shoulders had not fully relaxed since the morning his wife died.
Through the movement and camera flashes, Nova’s grin caught him mid-thought.
“Elliot Brooks?”
“That depends,” he said. “If you’re here to sell me a cable bundle, I’m not interested.”
Her smile widened.
“And if you’re the pop star,” he added, “I’m probably in trouble.”
She looked down at the floor briefly, but the smile stayed.
“Good,” she said. “I’d hate to start with a disappointment.”
He blinked, surprised into laughing with her.
Nova paused near the staircase, her gaze lifting toward the tall windows and chandelier overhead.
“It’s beautiful,” she said. “Big, but lived in.”
Her hand brushed along the polished banister as if testing whether the house was real or just another staged backdrop.
“Right here,” the photographer called. “Window light is perfect. Nova, lean in a little. Mr. Brooks, step beside her.”
Elliot moved into place before common sense could interfere.
The photographer circled them.
“Closer.”
Nova angled toward him, one shoulder brushing his arm.
He reminded himself it was only a photograph.
Details he hadn’t noticed a moment earlier began surfacing anyway.
The faint scent of salt air caught in her jacket. The warmth beneath the denim.
“Arm around her, Mr. Brooks.”
Elliot hesitated only a second before resting a careful hand against the middle of her back.
Dangerous complication, he thought immediately.
Too public.
The photographer snapped several quick shots.
“There we go. Stay right there.”
Nova tilted her head toward him.
“See?” she murmured. “You’re surviving.”
“Barely.”
Nova laughed, genuine this time, unguarded.
“Look at each other,” the photographer said.
Elliot obeyed before thinking better of it.
Her eyes met his.
Green. Steady. More tired than he had expected up close.
The camera clicked around them, but the noise no longer felt centered on either of them.
Then Nova blinked and stepped back first, almost as if she had remembered where she was.
The photographer lowered the camera.
“Oh, that is good,” he said. “Don’t lose that.”
Across the foyer, Marcy was no longer watching the lighting.
Nova glanced away, smoothing one hand along the sleeve of her jacket.
“Do you ever get used to this?” Elliot asked, nodding toward the equipment and movement surrounding them.
“You don’t,” she said. “You just get better at pretending it’s normal.”
The crew shifted toward the windows for another setup while cables slid across the floor.
Nova remained beside him instead of following.
Her gaze moved around the foyer again.
“Your house has a kind of peace to it,” she said finally. “Even with all this.”
“Mostly it’s just dust that’s learned to settle.”
A laugh escaped her.
“No,” she said. “It’s something else.”
For a moment, neither of them moved.
Then the photographer lifted the camera again.
“One more.”
Nova looked toward him this time instead of the lens.
And Elliot had the sudden, deeply inconvenient feeling that something inside the room had shifted.
“That’s enough,” Nova said, and the crew stopped moving.
Greg looked at his watch. “Nova—”
“Ten minutes,” she said without looking at him.
Something in her tone ended the discussion.
Reid lingered near the doorway a moment longer, studying Elliot with the calm attention of someone used to evaluating risk.
Elliot met the look directly.
After a second, Reid gave a small nod.
“I’ll be outside.”
“The most rebellious thing I’ve done this week was eat ice cream for dinner,” Elliot said. “I feel that should factor into your assessment.”
To Elliot’s surprise, Reid almost smiled before stepping out and closing the door behind him.
Silence settled over the house in slow layers after that.
Not complete silence.
The refrigerator hummed in the kitchen. Waves rolled somewhere beyond the back windows.
But compared to the last twenty minutes, it felt almost sacred.
Nova surveyed the foyer, the tension in her shoulders easing now that nobody was directing her where to stand.
“I hope we didn’t completely take over your morning.”
“It’s all right,” Elliot said. “Though I’m fairly certain my house now qualifies as a production studio.”
That drew another small laugh from her.
Her attention drifted toward the mantel.
A silver-framed photograph sat near the edge, beside an old clock.
A younger Elliot stood smiling beside a woman with warm eyes and a little girl tucked between them.
Nova’s attention settled on the picture.
“Your family?”
Elliot nodded once.
“My wife passed away five years ago.”
Saying it out loud still felt unfamiliar sometimes. Not sharp anymore. Just heavy in familiar places.
“I’m sorry,” she said.
And unlike most people, she didn’t rush to fill the silence after saying it.
That alone made him like her a little.
“You really don’t seem like my usual fan,” she said after a moment.
“Should I be fanboying more?”
Her mouth curved into a slight smile.
“Probably.”
She leaned against the edge of the mantel, studying him for a second.
“Most people either perform for me or panic around me.”
“And I’m doing neither?”
“You’re studying the exits,” she said.
“That’s habit.”
“Military?”
“Long time ago.”
She nodded as if that explained more than he intended to reveal.
Then her eyes returned to him again, thoughtful now.
“Most people want the fantasy,” she whispered. “You seem more interested in what’s real.”
Her words left a lingering impression on both of them.
Elliot let out a breath.
“I’m still not convinced this whole contest thing actually happened.”
She looked down, smiling before she could stop herself. 
For a moment, she looked nothing like the woman on the magazine covers.
“Well,” she said, “congratulations.”
He raised an eyebrow.
“You are officially my most accidental fan.”
“Glad to stand out.”
She didn’t look away immediately this time.
Not for the cameras.
“Oh,” she murmured. “This is going to be fun.”

Chapter 2
ELLIOT 
***
The house settled into a slower rhythm. Somewhere behind them, the surf eased against the shore. A gull passed overhead, its cry carrying through the air.
Nova tipped her chin toward the hallway. “We have what, eight minutes?”
Elliot checked the grandfather clock. “Seven, if your manager is punctual.”
“He is punctual about ruining fun.” She let out a small, conspiratorial smile. “Show me something they didn’t photograph.”
He almost said no. His house was not a showplace. The floors carried scuffs, the rugs had survived years of traffic, and the mugs never matched. Still, he motioned toward the archway. “This way.”
He led her into the den. Shelves lined one wall, filled with novels whose spines were sun-faded, a fine trace of dust catching the beam of light that slipped through the window. A low table held faint circles from forgotten coffee cups. On the mantel, a photograph rested at a slight angle, a young girl at the beach holding driftwood like a treasure, a woman’s hand reaching in from the side of the frame to take it.
“You have books,” she said softly. “I like that.”
“Old habits,” he said. “My wife, Sarah, used to call them clutter with bindings.”
A brief smile touched his mouth, gentle and worn. “The books stayed.”
“I’m sorry,” she said.
Nova’s gaze lingered on the photo a moment longer, then returned to him. She didn’t ask anything else.
He was grateful for that, and offered a small nod in return.
Her fingers trailed along the back of a chair. “Hotel rooms all look the same. I forget what real rooms feel like.”
He almost told her this one didn’t always feel like home to him either, not anymore, but he nodded toward the windows instead. “There is a better view in here.”
Elliot paused. “My life is not exactly a tour.”
“Good,” Nova said, her mouth curving. “I’m tired of rehearsals.”
She wandered a few steps farther into the room. Her fingertips traced the edge of a table beside a half-empty coffee mug. Her gaze moved to a worn Bible, the cover softened from years of use. She touched the corner lightly, as if testing the story behind it.
She glanced around again: the worn rug, the dip in the sofa cushion, books stacked unevenly on the side table.
“It’s real here,” she said softly. “Most places I walk into feel staged. This one doesn’t.”
“That is because it is,” he said. “I would not know how to fake it.”
“You can feel when a place has been prayed in.”
He looked at her. “You can?”
She lifted her eyes, offering a small, knowing smile. “You would be surprised by what people try to get when the noise fades. It gets quiet out there too. Just… different.”
He didn’t answer right away, as if the answer had landed somewhere he hadn’t expected. “Honest answer,” he said.
Her gaze moved around the room again, slower this time. “Surrounded and still alone,” she said. “Then maybe I should see more of it. Whatever makes this place feel the way it does.”
He studied her, then nodded toward the hallway. “You really want to see it?”
He led her to the glass doors at the far end of the den. They opened to a small balcony above the dunes. The ocean stretched wide and gray-blue, unhurried. 
“Okay,” she whispered. “That is an unfair view.”
“It came with the house.”
“You did not bargain for the ocean?”
“I am not much of a bargainer.”
She reached for the door handle at the same time he did.
Their hands brushed on the handle.
Just for a second.
But long enough for him to notice.
Nova looked toward the ocean after that, tucking a loose strand of hair behind her ear as if she suddenly needed somewhere else to focus.
Up close, the silver threaded through her auburn hair and the tiredness around her eyes became impossible to miss.
Older than the magazine covers suggested, and somehow more real because of it.
“Most of the places I stay don’t even open their windows,” she said quietly. “Here, it smells alive.”
“Maybe that is the ocean reminding you it exists,” he said.
Some of the performance slipped out of her expression.
“You notice things.”
“Sometimes,” he said, “when it matters.”
He expected her to step away after that.
She didn’t.
The moment stretched between them, filled with the distant wash of the surf and the cool salt air drifting through the open doorway.
Then Nova nodded toward the hallway.
“Come on,” she said lightly. “Before my time is up. I need to see if your coffee is as serious as your bookshelf.”
They moved back through the den to the kitchen. Sunlight stretched across the counters, glinting against the kettle and the row of mismatched mugs beside the sink.
Nova lifted herself onto the counter as if she belonged there already, boots dangling just above the hardwood.
“So, Elliot,” she said, swinging one foot lightly, “what do you recommend for somebody who’s forgotten what an actual meal tastes like?” 
He opened the refrigerator and studied the sparse contents.
“Probably another poor decision.”
He pulled out a foil-covered tin.
“Half a cherry pie,” he said. “Assuming time has not claimed it.”
“Perfect.” The teasing in her voice lingered longer than it should have. “Seven-minute pie date. I will take it.”
He peeled back the foil and handed her a fork.
Their fingers touched. Neither moved.
Warm enough to notice.
Nova broke through the crust with the edge of the fork and took a bite, her eyes closing briefly afterward.
“Most of what I eat lately comes in plastic packaging and gets handed to me in a car.”
She looked down at the pie again.
“This feels… nicer than it probably should.”
“Sugar and questionable judgment,” Elliot said.
“The best kind.”
She nudged the pie tin toward him.
“Your turn.”
He took a bite and considered it seriously enough to make her laugh again.
“That’s your review?” she asked. “You’re hard to impress.”
A small smile pulled at the corner of his mouth.
“Maybe I just don’t hand out reviews easily.”
Something thoughtful moved across her expression then.
“You don’t seem like someone who does anything easily.”
Elliot leaned lightly against the counter.
“That sounds dangerously close to psychoanalysis.”
“Occupational hazard.”
“Pop star?”
“Human observation.”
The answer caught him off guard enough that he looked away briefly.
“Tell me something real,” she said. “Not favorite-color real. Actual real.”
He thought about it.
“I read the Bible every morning,” he said. “I burn toast at a statistically impressive rate. And I tried jogging on the beach once. The beach won.”
Nova laughed fully this time, her head tipping back slightly.
“That makes sense,” she said. “Sand is absolutely a scam.”
Elliot glanced toward the clock near the stove.
“You are down to five minutes.”
“Then stop hiding behind the clock.”
Her tone stayed light, but the words landed differently anyway.
“This is your home,” she said. “What do you actually like about this place? Oceanside.”
Elliot shrugged slightly.
“It minds its business. You can walk the boardwalk and nobody cares if you buy seashell magnets in your fifties.”
She smirked.
“Do you buy seashell magnets in your fifties?”
“Only on reckless occasions. Sometimes I even buy taffy.”
“Dangerous.”
Her eyes warmed again before she looked toward the kitchen window, toward the narrow square of pale sky beyond it.
“My turn,” she said more quietly. “Something real.”
She hesitated just long enough for him to notice it.
“I like this better than stadium lights.”
“And,” she added with faint humor, “the attention of fans isn’t the same thing as being seen.”
The words settled into the room between them.
Nova tapped her fingers lightly against the countertop before giving him a smaller smile.
“Anyway,” she said, “you are an excellent host.”
“All I did was give you pie I probably should not even own.”
“Exactly,” she said. “Authentic.”
She set the fork down beside the tin.
“Next time we skip the small talk.”
Elliot raised an eyebrow.
“Honestly, I trust people who lead with pie.”
Her hand settled on the counter beside his.
Then stayed there.
The warmth of her fingers settled against the back of his hand.
After a second, her pinky shifted slightly against his.
Small and careful.
Not accidental.
He didn’t move either.
The contact lingered between them until the knock came from the foyer.
“Nova. Schedule,” Greg called.
Her hand slipped away immediately.
The absence of her hand felt noticeable.
Elliot straightened slightly, pretending his attention had already shifted toward the hallway.
Nova smoothed one sleeve of her jacket, the polished composure from earlier slipping back into place almost instantly.
“Duty calls,” she said.
She hopped down from the counter, though when she looked back at him, the expression in her eyes softened again.
“Thanks for not making this weird.”
Elliot let out a quiet breath.
“I am doing my best not to understand any part of this.”
“Keep doing that,” she said. “The not trying part.”
She took one final bite of pie before setting the fork down carefully beside the tin.
“Nova,” Greg called again, closer now.
She started toward the doorway, then stopped halfway there.
“There is a local experience thing included in this contest, right?” she asked. “The fine print nobody reads?”
Elliot blinked once.
“Possibly.”
“Good.”
She pointed lightly toward him.
“I am cashing it in.”
“That sounds mildly threatening.”
“Breakfast tomorrow,” she said, ignoring that. “Somewhere normal. No cameras. You pick.”
Elliot opened his mouth, several reasonable objections arriving all at once.
She was already moving again before he could say any of them.
“Greg will text you with the details,” she called over her shoulder. “He’ll pretend this was his idea.”
She reached the door and glanced back one last time.
“For the record,” she said, “that was a superb pie date.”
Elliot looked down at the pie tin, unable to hide the reaction entirely.
“I will add it to my résumé.”
Her grin widened.
“Careful. You will get requests for sequels.”
He watched her pull the door open, ocean wind catching the edge of her jacket again.
“I’m coming, Greg,” she called, her voice brighter now.
Then quieter:
“See you tomorrow, Elliot.”
The door closed behind her.
Silence settled back through the kitchen in slow layers.
The refrigerator hummed softly.
Somewhere beyond the windows, waves rolled against the shore.
Elliot looked down at the half-finished pie, the two forks resting beside each other, the faint warmth still lingering where her hand had touched his.
For the first time in years, he found himself wondering if God answered prayers in ways people never thought to ask for.
“Breakfast,” he murmured. “Right.”
Ten minutes later, his phone buzzed.
Remember. Breakfast tomorrow. You choose. — N
Not Greg’s number. Hers.
Elliot stared at the screen a moment before typing back.
Okay. 9 a.m. Harbor Light Cafe. Good coffee. Terrible chairs. 
Need to be done by ten. Church. 
Her reply appeared almost immediately.
Perfect. I like terrible chairs. Keeps a girl humble. 
And I promise not to make you late for church. 
He laughed softly under his breath, saved her number as “N,” and set the phone face down on the counter.
Around him, the house slowly settled back into itself.
He told himself tomorrow would be easy.
As if saying it might make it true.

Chapter 3
I used to sing beneath the trees,
Barefoot dreams in the summer breeze.
No stage, no lights, nothing to prove,
Just sound and sky, and room to move.
- Nova Hart, from “Before the Noise” 12 weeks at #1 
NOVA
***
It was not easy. Not at all.
Nova tightened both hands on the steering wheel as the rented SUV rolled past the marina for the second time.
She’d booked it herself, the night before, before she could talk herself out of it.
Harbor Light Café sat just ahead, tucked beside the boardwalk beneath a faded blue sign that looked older than half the buildings around it.
She still had time to leave.
That was the problem.
At a red light, she glanced down at the open text thread and typed before she could reconsider it.
Running a few late. Didn't tell Reid.
He would've followed me.
No rush. Chairs are still terrible.
Perfect.
Have not driven a car myself in months!
Nova laughed.
Five minutes later, she parked beside the marina and killed the engine.
For a moment, she stayed there listening to gulls somewhere beyond the boardwalk and the soft rattle of the wind against the SUV.
Then she pulled the hood a little lower and stepped out into the salt air.
Warm light glowed through the diner windows.
She spotted Elliot immediately.
He looked like the kind of man who arrived early to places.
He sat in the corner booth with both hands around a coffee mug, calm and completely at home in the low hum of the diner.
She opened the door.
The small bell above it jingled softly.
A few people glanced up before returning to their breakfasts.
Elliot looked up last.
His expression changed the moment he saw her.
Warmth.
Relief maybe.
Something she wanted to see again.
She crossed toward the booth, morning light catching the edge of her hair beneath the ball cap.
"You made it," he said, starting to stand out of reflex.
She slid into the opposite side first. "Wouldn't miss it. Besides, I owed you a normal breakfast."
"Normal might be ambitious," he replied, smiling.
Elliot's hands settled around his mug. "So. This is … surreal."
"Which part? The pie for breakfast guy or the pop star who bribed her manager with airline miles to get an extra morning in town?"
He blinked once. "You bribed him?"
"I prefer negotiated. I told him I would do the late-night talk show in Los Angeles if he gave me a real morning here. And by real, I mean one that does not involve lighting, excessive makeup, or microphones."
He shook his head. "Seems like a high price for coffee."
"That is how you know it is worth it," she said. "Besides, you promised terrible chairs. I am here for the full local experience."
Elliot's gaze moved over her briefly before settling back on his coffee.
In daylight, she looked less polished than she had on camera yesterday. Freckles showed beneath the makeup she no longer wore, and exhaustion blurred the polished edges the world usually saw.
Nova turned her mug slowly between her hands. "You know the funny part? We did the whole official shoot at your house yesterday. Lights, crew, the works. Greg was thrilled with the pictures."
Elliot nodded once, waiting.
"Then Greg took one look at them later and vetoed the whole batch."
Elliot raised an eyebrow. "That bad?"
A faint smile touched her mouth. "His exact words were: 'These don't look promotional anymore.'"
Elliot set his mug down carefully but didn't say anything.
Nova glanced toward the window, her expression softening. "Unscripted moments tend to get expensive."
Elliot leaned back slightly. "So the staged photos stay private, but the terrible-chair breakfast is the dangerous one?"
"Exactly," she said with a low laugh. "The internet likes the unscripted version best."
A waitress approached with her order pad and a pot of coffee, smiling in that half-curious, half-knowing way only small-town waitresses seemed capable of perfecting.
"Morning, Doreen," Elliot said.
"Morning, hon." She turned to Nova, already pouring. "Coffee?"
Nova took a sip, winced immediately, then grinned. "Wow. That is strong enough to qualify as a medical procedure."
Doreen laughed. "House blend."
Then she glanced toward Elliot with a teasing look. "Thought you said you weren't expecting anyone, Mr. Brooks."
Elliot nearly choked on his coffee. "Guess I was mistaken."
"Lying to waitresses now?" Doreen topped off his mug with a smirk. "Bold strategy for a first date."
"It is not a date," Elliot said, though the denial arrived thinner than intended.
Doreen gave him a patient look. "Honey, I've worked breakfast shifts for twenty-three years."
Nova pressed her lips together, trying not to laugh.
Then Doreen turned to Nova.
Nova felt her shoulders tighten.
For a second she expected the shift: the excitement, the questions, the sudden change people always made once they realized who she was.
Instead, Doreen stepped back toward the booth and lowered her voice.
"Don't worry, sweetheart," she said gently. "Your secret's safe with me."
Nova blinked.
"I believe you."
Doreen patted the edge of the table once before straightening. "Enjoy your breakfast, honey."
Elliot watched the exchange over his coffee, amused. "Even in disguise, you still get recognized."
Nova stirred cream into her cup. "Yeah. But at least she feels like somebody who knows how to keep a secret."
"Most people here do," he said. "Part of the charm."
"At least the locals," she said.
"Then maybe you picked the right café."
"Maybe I did."
Nova leaned back slightly in the booth. "So tell me something, Elliot. What exactly is your deal? You look like somebody who alphabetizes his spice rack."
"I do," he admitted. "Except for paprika. I cannot commit to where it belongs."
Nova frowned thoughtfully. "Technically, isn't paprika just ambitious pepper?"
Elliot stared at her for a second. "That may be the most upsetting thing anyone has ever said in this diner."
"Wild man."
"Living dangerously," he agreed.
"And you?" he asked. "Do pop stars alphabetize?"
The humor faded slightly from her expression.
"I don't even unpack anymore," she said. "Half the time I don't know what somebody packed for me."
Elliot studied her over the rim of his mug.
"I don't think that's supposed to sound as lonely as it does."
The honesty of it caught her off guard enough that she looked away.
"People expect shiny," she said eventually, tracing one finger along the edge of her mug. "Nobody really knows what to do with normal."
Elliot rested his elbows against the table. "You get tired of being expected all the time?"
Nova laughed once under her breath. "I get tired of pretending it's easy."
Silence settled between them.
"Yesterday slowed down for a minute," she admitted.
Elliot frowned slightly. "When?"
She looked down, composure slipping.
"The pie."
Heat climbed into his expression almost instantly.
"And the quiet," she added. "You didn't rush to fill it."
Elliot leaned back. "I'm not especially gifted at filling things. Conversations. Empty rooms. Holes in drywall…"
Doreen returned with a pen poised above her pad. "So, what are we having this morning?"
Nova handed back the menu she had barely opened. "Pancakes, bacon, and those crispy potatoes that make people rethink their life choices."
"Home fries," Doreen corrected, scribbling it down. "Good choice."
Elliot closed his menu. "Eggs and toast. Predictable but safe."
"Nothing wrong with safe," Doreen said before heading toward the kitchen.
Nova watched her go, then looked back at him. "You do not strike me as a man who is actually safe."
He lifted an eyebrow. "No?"
"You act steady," she said, studying him over the rim of her mug. "But I bet there's a little chaos tucked under the collar."
He smiled. "Chaos feels generous. These days I'm mostly routine and tax deductions."
"Please." Her expression drifted into amusement. "You were halfway charming before you started sounding like an accountant."
"Halfway?"
She leaned forward. "You'd probably get the other half if you stopped trying so hard to be predictable."
Elliot laughed, low and real. "You think I'm trying to be boring?"
"Definitely."
"That harsh?"
"That observant."
He tilted his head, amused. "And you figured that out before breakfast arrived?"
Nova smirked. "I'm good at reading people. Comes with the job."
Elliot opened his mouth to answer, but Doreen appeared with two steaming plates balanced along one arm.
"Here we are," she said brightly. "Pancakes, bacon, eggs, and what looks suspiciously like a first date."
Elliot opened his mouth, reconsidered, and reached for his coffee instead.
Doreen smirked as she slid the plates onto the table. "Honey, I was born knowing a first date when I see one."
Nova hid a grin behind her coffee cup.
"We appreciate the service, Doreen," she said lightly.
"Mm-hmm." Doreen straightened. "Enjoy yourselves, sweethearts."
Elliot waited until she disappeared toward the counter before exhaling. "You did not have to encourage her."
"Sure I did." Nova reached for the syrup. "Watching you panic has been the highlight of my morning."
He shook his head, faint color rising into his face. "Then I should probably tip better."
"Good answer."
Nova drowned her pancakes in syrup, then winced.
"Too much. Proof I shouldn't adult before noon."
Elliot smiled into his coffee. "You overthink syrup more than most people do their actual life choices."
"I have a complicated relationship with breakfast," she said, cutting into the pancakes. "Every tour morning some nutritionist lectures me about fueling my instrument. I just want pancakes."
Elliot watched her over the rim of his mug, smiling at her.
She caught him looking. "What?"
"Nothing," he said. "Just collecting data."
"Data?"
"You talk faster when you're nervous, you overthink syrup, and you enjoy pretending you're not famous."
She tilted her head. "You think you have me figured out after one awkward photo shoot and half a breakfast?"
"Half a breakfast is plenty of data."
Nova smirked. "You're confident for someone who looks mildly terrified."
"I'm not terrified," he said. "Just aware the world might implode if people realize you're having pancakes with a middle-aged man."
"Then let's make a deal. You don't act starstruck, and I won't sign your napkin."
"Deal," he said. "Though if you did, I might auction it later."
Her grin widened. "You're getting better at this."
"At what?"
"Not pretending you dislike me."
"That's usually how breakfast works."
He laughed. "You really don't have an off switch, do you?"
"Not when the conversation's good."
"Apparently I smiled first."

It was quiet, the way he was quiet. Unhurried. Certain. Nothing performed.
She did not step back.
They stood together on the boardwalk with the ocean moving beside them. She was simply present and unhidden, in a faded sweatshirt that smelled like the sea.
That was enough.
More than.
***
That evening, the house settled into its familiar rhythm.
Clara had gone home. The dishes were done. The coffee maker was set for morning.
Nova sat on the back porch steps with her knees pulled up. Elliot came outside and sat beside her. Not performing closeness. Just there.
The weight of it settled between them. Not burden. Ballast.
The ocean spread out before them, dark and patient. The horizon held its line. Above it, the first stars were appearing, not all at once but one by one, the way honest things tend to arrive.
"Soon," she had said, once, standing in this same house, on her way out the door.
"Soon," he had answered.
It had taken longer than either of them expected.
It had cost more than either of them had planned.
But here, in the dark, with the ocean steady and the stars arriving one by one.
Soon had come.

ELLIOT
***
He noticed the second mug sometime in November.
Not when it happened. He noticed it later, on a morning when he reached for his without looking and his hand found hers first, smaller and handle-out, on the right side of the drying rack where his had always been. He stood there with the kettle in his hand for a second. Then he moved it to the left and poured the coffee.
After that he stopped moving it.
The boardwalk below had gone quiet for winter and was only now beginning to come back. A few early joggers. A family with a stroller moving slow against the wind. The café had kept its usual hours, but Doreen had told him in January that February was always the honest month, when you found out who actually liked the town and who had only liked the weather.
He poured two mugs.
Nova was in the other room. He could hear the low sound of her working. Not music exactly, more like the shape of it, something she was finding. She had been finding it since before he came downstairs. He knew because he had heard her at the door while he was still upstairs, the quiet click of the latch, and by the time he reached the kitchen her coat was already on the hook, her shoes near the door in the particular way she had when she let herself in without knocking.
He did not call out. He set her mug on the counter where she would see it when she came through.
He drank his coffee at the kitchen table and watched the water.
The tide was going out. A pelican moved along the shoreline in that way pelicans had, low and unbothered, as if it had somewhere specific to be and had decided not to rush. Elliot watched it until it disappeared behind the pier.
Clara let herself in at half past nine.
She set her bag near the door, glanced at Nova’s coat on the hook, and came into the kitchen without ceremony.
“Morning,” she said.
“Morning.” He was already reaching for a third mug.
She sat at the table and wrapped both hands around the coffee he set in front of her. The wind moved through the sea oats on the dune below. Neither of them spoke for a moment, which was not uncomfortable, which was the way it had gotten to be.
Nova came through from the hallway with her phone in one hand and a look on her face that meant she had just figured something out and was not sure whether to be pleased or skeptical about it.
She stopped when she saw Clara.
“Oh,” she said. “Hi.”
“Hi.” Clara did not elaborate. She drank her coffee.
Nova looked at Elliot. He offered nothing useful.
She sat down.
For a while the three of them were at the table with their mugs and the morning light coming through the window and the sound of the water below. It was not a conversation exactly. It was something quieter than that.
Clara finished her coffee and stood. She carried her mug to the sink, rinsed it, and set it on the left side of the drying rack. Then she picked up her bag.
“I have papers to grade,” she said.
“On a Saturday,” Nova said.
“Math doesn’t care about Saturday.”
She said goodbye and then she was gone, the door clicking behind her, and the kitchen was the way it had been before.
Nova looked at her mug. “That was almost normal.”
“It was,” Elliot said.
She looked at him. Something moved across her face and settled. He had learned to let those moments pass without naming them.
He got up and refilled both mugs without being asked.
The song had been out since February. He knew because Greg had sent a single text about streaming numbers, which Elliot had read twice and set his phone face down and gone back to what he was doing. He did not follow the charts. He had heard it twice: once when she played it in this kitchen, and once when it came through the speakers at the grocery store and the cashier, a teenager, had looked up at him and then looked away without saying anything, which was its own kind of grace.
Doreen had brought it up once, the week after the release.
“Heard that song of hers,” she had said, setting down his coffee. “You the one she wrote it for?”
“I don’t know,” he had said.
Doreen had given him the look she reserved for people being deliberately obtuse. Then she had gone to take someone else’s order.
Nova was quiet in the chair across from him, looking out at the water. She had pulled her feet up under her. She was wearing his socks, he noticed, which was not a new development but was still something he noticed.
He thought about what it had cost her to get here. Not to Oceanside. To this: the chair, the coat on the hook that no one mentioned, the melody she had been finding since before his first cup was poured.
Perfect love drives out fear. He had read those words in Sarah’s handwriting, circled in her Bible, months after she was gone. He had not understood them then.
He thought he was getting closer.
Nova looked over and caught him watching.
“What?” she said.
“Nothing,” he said. “Just collecting data.”
She gave him a look that told him she remembered. Then she turned back to the water.
The tide was coming back in now. The pelican had returned, or another one, moving along the line where the waves dissolved into foam, indifferent to everything.
He had asked himself, once, what it would mean for the noise to be worth something. Whether love could justify the exposure, the way his name had appeared in places he had never wanted it to appear, the reporters who had stood at the end of his street for three weeks and then one day simply were not there anymore.
He did not ask himself that anymore.
The morning was not his. It had not been for some time. It belonged to the two mugs on the drying rack and the coat on the hook and the sound of someone finding a melody at first light and a daughter who let herself in without calling and sat for twelve minutes and called it a visit.
Thank You, he said, not out loud, just the shape of it, the kind of prayer that did not require words to reach where it was going.
Outside, the town was waking up. Below the boardwalk, the ocean moved the way it always had, without opinion, without season, the same sound it had made the morning Nova Hart first walked through his door.
She was still here.
That was new.
That was everything.
