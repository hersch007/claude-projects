# Course 2: AMP for Government / Jurisdiction Reviewers
## Lesson Outline for Review

**Audience:** Local Government staff (Clients/Jurisdictions) + WTS Analysts  
**Prerequisite:** Course 1 -- AMP Fundamentals (all 6 lessons)  
**Estimated Duration:** 90--120 minutes  
**Format:** 7 lessons, each with SOP instruction + scenario-based checkpoint question

---

## Course Objective

Equip Jurisdiction staff and WTS Analysts to confidently navigate AMP, evaluate submitted applications, make legally defensible decisions, manage the Shot Clock, and maintain a complete Official Record -- from the moment an application is submitted through final project closure.

## Course Outcomes

Upon completion, users will be able to:
- Understand their legal authority and responsibility as a Jurisdiction reviewer
- Navigate the dashboard and immediately identify which projects require action
- Review the PIF and determine whether to approve or request revision
- Evaluate technical components (NWF, TWF, NCM, ATF) using the correct standards
- Manage the Shot Clock and avoid federal deemed-approval consequences
- Make fully documented approval, revision, and denial decisions
- Conduct and close out final inspections
- Keep all communications inside AMP to maintain the Official Record

## Lesson 1: Your Authority and Responsibility as a Jurisdiction Reviewer

### Learning Objectives
- State the legal authority of the Jurisdiction in the AMP review process
- Distinguish between what the Jurisdiction decides and what WTS/Analysts support
- Explain the legal consequences of the decisions made in AMP
- Understand the difference between Client and Analyst roles at the decision level

### Key Topics
1. **What you are responsible for** -- Every project in your jurisdiction is yours to approve, revise, or deny. No one else can exercise that authority on your behalf.
2. **Your legal exposure** -- Jurisdiction decisions on wireless facility applications carry federal weight. Decisions not supported by an Official Record can be challenged and reversed.
3. **The Client vs. Analyst distinction** -- Analysts review for completeness and flag issues. You review for compliance and make the call. The Analyst's role is advisory only.
4. **What WTS does NOT do** -- WTS does not approve applications, does not represent carriers, does not modify ordinance requirements.
5. **Your jurisdiction's ordinance** -- Your local ordinance defines the specific requirements for wireless facility applications in your area. AMP enforces it; you interpret it.
6. **Accountability through the Official Record** -- Every action you take in AMP is logged. Your decision will have your name, your timestamp, and your written rationale in the permanent record.
7. **What happens when decisions are wrong** -- Federal preemption, Shot Clock violations, and inadequately documented denials -- the legal consequences of errors.

### Checkpoint Question Type
Scenario: An Analyst flags a structural analysis as incomplete. The Jurisdiction reviewer approves the component anyway without documenting why. The carrier builds and there is later a structural failure. What is the Jurisdiction's legal position?

## Lesson 2: Reading the Dashboard -- Workload Management and Priority

### Learning Objectives
- Log in and immediately identify which projects require action
- Read the full dashboard row correctly (never one column in isolation)
- Distinguish between projects that need immediate action vs. those being monitored
- Identify Shot Clock urgency from the dashboard view
- Understand how dashboard view differs by role (Client vs. Analyst vs. Agent)

### Key Topics
1. **What you see when you log in** -- The Jurisdiction reviewer sees ALL projects in their jurisdiction. Agents see only their own. Understanding why this matters for oversight.
2. **Reading the full row** -- Every column tells you something. Status alone is not enough. You must scan the entire row: project type, carrier, Shot Clock remaining, last activity, component status.
3. **The Shot Clock column** -- What the number means. When to be concerned. At what point is action urgent.
4. **Red-status projects: immediate priority** -- Any project in Revise, AIT Revise, or Awaiting Revision requires your direct attention. Do not let red-status projects sit.
5. **Pending and Invoiced: monitor only** -- These are moving through the system. Your job is to confirm they progress, not to intervene.
6. **Filtering and prioritizing your queue** -- How to manage a full dashboard across multiple active projects.
7. **Common dashboard misreads** -- What happens when reviewers only look at one column, assume green means done, or miss that a red-status project is waiting on them.

## Lesson 3: Reviewing the Project Information Form (PIF)

### Learning Objectives
- Identify what the PIF contains and why each field matters for review
- Evaluate whether a PIF is complete and accurate
- Understand how the project type selection affects the entire downstream review
- Approve a PIF or issue a documented revision request
- Know what happens after PIF approval (components activate)

### Key Topics
1. **What the PIF tells you** -- Project type, location, jurisdiction assignment, carrier identity, project description. These fields define everything that follows.
2. **The project type decision** -- The most critical field in the system. It determines required components, fees, Shot Clock length, and regulatory pathway. Errors here cascade through the entire project.
3. **PIF completeness checklist** -- What a properly completed PIF contains. What missing or inconsistent fields look like.
4. **Common PIF errors** -- Wrong project type selected, mismatched site information, missing carrier identification, incorrect jurisdiction assignment.
5. **Approving the PIF** -- What "approve" means at this stage: you have confirmed the project type, site, and jurisdiction are correct. You are not approving the tower -- you are approving the application to proceed.
6. **Requesting PIF revision** -- How to issue a documented revision request. What specificity is required. What you cannot request ("just fix it" is not sufficient).
7. **After PIF approval** -- Components activate. Fees are calculated. The Shot Clock begins or continues. Work can now begin on component forms.

## Lesson 4: Reviewing Technical Components

### Learning Objectives
- Know what each component (NWF, TWF, NCM, ATF) contains and requires
- Identify what a properly completed component looks like vs. a deficient one
- Apply the correct standard for structural analysis review (NCM)
- Issue specific, documented revision requests for deficient components
- Know when a component issue rises to the level of denial vs. revision

### Key Topics
1. **Component overview** -- Quick refresher: PIF + ATF + NWF + TWF + NCM. Which project types require which components.
2. **What you are reviewing for** -- You are not engineering the tower. You are verifying that the required certified professionals have completed and stamped the required documentation, and that it is complete and consistent.
3. **The NCM: New Colocation/Modification (required for ALL projects)**
   - The structural analysis: what it must contain
   - PE certification: must be licensed in the state where the tower is located
   - Loading standards: what 100% loading means and why the threshold matters
   - What a properly stamped structural report looks like vs. a deficient one
   - Red flags: missing PE stamp, out-of-state license, incomplete loading analysis
4. **The NWF: New Wireless Facility** (New Tower projects only)
   - Site justification requirements
   - Visual impact documentation
   - What the Jurisdiction can and cannot require under federal law
5. **The TWF: Tower/Wireless Facility Registration**
   - When this is required (tower not current in WTS database)
   - What tower data must be current
6. **The ATF: Add Tower Form**
   - When used: new tower site being added to the database
   - What baseline information is required
7. **Document standards** -- What makes a supporting document acceptable: must be signed, stamped, current, and consistent with project data.
8. **Requesting component revision** -- Specific deficiency required. "This structural analysis is missing the PE license number for the reviewing engineer" is acceptable. "The documents look incomplete" is not.

## Lesson 5: The Shot Clock -- Federal Deadline Management

### Learning Objectives
- State the Shot Clock length for each project type
- Explain what happens when a Shot Clock expires (deemed approval)
- Identify when the clock can be stopped and what triggers a stop
- Track Shot Clock status on the dashboard
- Know what to do when a project is approaching its deadline

### Key Topics
1. **Shot Clock lengths by project type** (from federal regulation)
   - Standard applications (New Tower, Colocation, Modification): 150 days
   - Small Wireless Facility (SWF) -- single site: 60 days
   - Small Wireless Facility (SWF) -- batch (up to 25 sites): 90 days
2. **What "deemed approved" actually means** -- The carrier can build. The Jurisdiction cannot stop it. This is not a penalty -- it is a federal legal consequence that is very difficult to reverse.
3. **When does the clock start?** -- From submission of a complete application. AMP tracks this automatically from the submission date.
4. **Can the clock be stopped?** -- Yes, under specific conditions: when the Jurisdiction issues a completeness determination showing the application is incomplete. Once the carrier corrects and resubmits, the clock restarts or continues.
5. **The incompleteness stop-clock: how to use it correctly** -- The stop must be for a genuine documented deficiency, not a delay tactic. Federal courts have addressed this. Using incompleteness findings improperly creates additional legal risk.
6. **Monitoring clock status in AMP** -- The dashboard column. What the numbers mean. When to escalate internally.
7. **What to do when the clock is close** -- Escalation procedures. Contacting your WTS Analyst. Making a decision vs. requesting revision at day 140+.
8. **AMP's role in Shot Clock enforcement** -- AMP does not stop the clock automatically. The Jurisdiction must take action within AMP for the clock stop to be documented.

### Checkpoint Question Type
Scenario: A reviewer realizes that a standard New Tower application has been sitting in her queue for 148 days. She has not yet made a completeness determination and has not approved or denied the project. What is her legal exposure, and what can she do right now?

## Lesson 6: Making Decisions -- Approvals, Revisions, and Denials

### Learning Objectives
- Apply the three-option decision framework: approve, revise, or deny
- Write a valid, specific revision request
- Identify legally permissible grounds for denial
- Identify what federal law prohibits as grounds for denial
- Document all decisions correctly in AMP so they are defensible

### Key Topics
1. **The decision framework** -- Every component and every project stage results in one of three outcomes: approve, request revision, or deny. There is no "pending indefinitely" option.
2. **When to approve** -- The application meets all requirements. Documentation is complete. Certifications are valid. Project type is correct. The Shot Clock has not expired.
3. **When to request revision** -- A specific, documented deficiency exists that the Agent can correct. The revision request must name the exact deficiency, not make vague requests.
   - Good: "The structural analysis is missing the PE license number."
   - Not acceptable: "Please provide additional documentation."
4. **The revision cycle and the Shot Clock** -- Issuing a revision request may affect the Shot Clock depending on whether it constitutes a completeness finding. Know the difference.
5. **When to deny** -- Denial is not the first option for minor deficiencies. Reserve denial for applications that cannot be corrected because they violate substantive requirements.
6. **What you CAN deny on** (under federal law and local ordinance)
   - Structural safety (inadequate engineering that cannot be corrected without redesign)
   - Zoning violations that cannot be cured by modification
   - Incomplete application after proper notice and opportunity to cure
7. **What you CANNOT deny on** -- This is critical. Federal law prohibits certain denial grounds:
   - RF/EMF emissions concerns when the carrier has demonstrated FCC compliance
   - The mere presence of wireless facilities in the community
   - Unreasonable discrimination between carriers of the same service type
8. **How denial must be documented** -- A denial without a written record is a denial that will not survive challenge. The decision must be in writing, citing specific grounds, in the Official Record.
9. **The Analyst's role in your decision** -- Analysts can recommend and flag. They cannot decide. Your approval is your decision and your record.

### Checkpoint Question Type
Scenario: A community member has called the Jurisdiction's office to complain about a new cell tower application near a school, citing concerns about RF radiation exposure. The permitting officer wants to deny the application based on these concerns. The carrier has provided current FCC RF compliance documentation. What should the Jurisdiction do?

## Lesson 7: Communication, Final Inspection, and Project Closure

### Learning Objectives
- Communicate with Agents and Analysts exclusively through AMP
- Understand what "final inspection" requires and who conducts it
- Process an inspection result in AMP
- Close a project and understand what Completed status means
- Know the record retention obligations for your jurisdiction

### Key Topics
1. **The all-in-AMP communication rule** -- Everything goes through AMP. No email, no phone call, no side conversation creates a valid record. If it did not happen in AMP, it did not happen legally.
2. **Communicating with Agents in AMP** -- How to use the messaging/comment system. What is appropriate to communicate. What constitutes an official revision request vs. an informal note.
3. **Working with your WTS Analyst inside AMP** -- How Analyst notes and recommendations appear. How to use Analyst support without delegating your authority.
4. **What the final inspection is** -- On-site verification that what was built matches what was approved. This is not a WTS function -- it is typically conducted by the Jurisdiction's building department or a designated inspector.
5. **What the inspector checks** -- As-built configuration must match approved application. Key verification points: structure type, antenna configuration, carrier identity.
6. **Logging inspection results in AMP** -- Pass, fail, conditional pass. What triggers each outcome. What happens if the as-built does not match the approved application.
7. **Project closure: the Completed status** -- When a project reaches Completed, it means all stages are verified and the project is permanently closed. The Official Record is finalized.
8. **Record retention** -- The Official Record in AMP is permanent. However, your jurisdiction may have additional obligations under state law for record retention of permits and approvals. AMP does not replace those obligations -- it fulfills the federal documentation requirement.
